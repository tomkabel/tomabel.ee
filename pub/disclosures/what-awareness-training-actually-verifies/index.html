What awareness training actually verifies

Cyber awareness training is bought as evidence of resilience. Under NIS2 and DORA it is the line item an auditor asks for. What a training platform can measure is who completed which task and what risk score the course assigned them. Whether an attack against that person fails is a different quantity, and two large field studies say the first does not predict the second. A worked example from an Estonian vendor whose public methodology is detailed enough to test.

The claim and the measurement

A security control has two descriptions. One is what it claims to verify. The other is what it actually verifies. Most of the work on this site lives in the gap between the two, usually at the protocol or opcode layer. This piece applies the same question to a control that runs on people.

Awareness training claims to verify that an organisation's staff will not fall for an attack. What the platform can observe is narrower: a task was opened, a task was completed, an answer was given, a risk score was computed from those answers. None of those observations is an attack failing. The distance between 'completed the module' and 'the phish bounced off' is the whole question, and in most procurement it is never measured.

This matters in Estonia for a plain reason. People in Estonia lost over 29 million euros to fraud in 2025, against 16 million in 2024, with phone scams accounting for about 11.5 million of the total, according to the Police and Border Guard Board. The Information System Authority's yearbook for the same year carries the same 29 million figure. The fraud essay on this site traces how that happened. This essay asks what the organisational control bought against it actually verifies.

What the law asks for

NIS2 requires essential and important entities to take measures that include "basic cyber hygiene practices and cybersecurity training" (Article 21(2)(g)). It also requires members of management bodies to "follow training", and asks Member States to encourage similar training for employees "on a regular basis" (Article 20(2)). DORA requires financial entities to "develop ICT security awareness programmes and digital operational resilience training as compulsory modules in their staff training schemes" (Article 13(6)).

Read those three provisions again for the word they do not contain. None of them requires the training to work. They require it to exist, to recur and to be documented. An auditor checking NIS2 Article 21(2)(g) is checking for a programme, not for an outcome. That is not a criticism of the law. It is the reason a completion certificate satisfies the audit, and the reason completion is what gets measured.

What the field studies measure instead

Lain, Kostiainen and Capkun ran a 15-month study at a partner company from July 2019 to October 2020, with 14,773 employees as participants, published as an arXiv preprint in December 2021. They measured click rates, credential submission and macro enabling on simulated phishing in the employees' normal work context. On embedded training, the kind shown to an employee right after they click a simulated phish, their finding is stated without hedging: it "does not make employees more resilient to phishing, but instead it can have unexpected side effects that can make employees even more susceptible to phishing." Two things did work in that study. Warnings on suspicious emails were effective. And a reporting button turned the workforce into a detection sensor: new campaigns were reported "within few minutes from their launch", and the employees kept reporting over the full 15 months.

Ho and colleagues ran an 8-month randomised controlled experiment at a large healthcare organisation, with ten simulated phishing campaigns sent to over 19,500 employees, published at the IEEE Symposium on Security and Privacy in 2025. The abstract reports "no significant relationship between whether users have recently completed cybersecurity awareness training and their likelihood of failing a phishing simulation." For embedded training, "the absolute difference in failure rates between trained and untrained users is extremely low across a variety of training content." Most users "spend minimal time interacting with embedded phishing training material", and for some content types, users who completed more training "can have an increased likelihood of failing subsequent phishing simulations." The authors' conclusion is that such programmes "in their current and commonly deployed forms, are unlikely to offer significant practical value in reducing phishing risks."

The limit of both studies is the same. They tested the forms of training deployed in those two organisations, under simulated phishing designed by the researchers. A different programme, measured the same way, could produce a different number. The point is that the number can be produced. Completion rate and a self-assessed risk score are not that number.

RiskSight, as published on risksight.io on 2026-11-12

RiskSight's About page gives its address as "Pirni 7/1, Tallinn 10617, Estonia". I have no affiliation with RiskSight, commercial or personal. They are the example here because they are an Estonian vendor whose public methodology is detailed enough to test, which is a compliment to their transparency. The findings are not softened to compensate.

The evidence is a frozen snapshot of 52 English pages from risksight.io, fetched on 2026-11-12, with a SHA-256 per file. Every sentence attributed to RiskSight below is a verbatim quotation from that snapshot, checked by script, or a count computed over it. Nothing here names their clients, although the home page lists several. Nothing is said about revenue, staff or motives, because the snapshot says nothing checkable about those.

Start with what the product is sold as. The home page reads: "Your partner for building the human firewall." The product page lists the benefits, and one of them is headed "Evidence for NIS2, DORA and ISO 27001", explained as "A documented, continuous programme that showcases compliance to auditors and supervisory authorities." The outcome benefit is "Regular cyber hygiene training cuts click-through on malicious emails and credential reuse." The reference case on the home page is an "Annual cyber awareness training programme since 2017 in six countries for 500 to 700 users with a visibly decreasing risk level."

Now what is measured. The product FAQ answers the question directly: "Progress is measured on a completion and risk basis. Analysts monitor completion rates and overdue users are flagged and notified. Similarly, analysts monitor risk levels and are notified of high-risk users." The risk level comes from inside the course: "Risk is assessed in 12 threat vectors which are categorised into four classes – knowledge, exposure, belonging, and personality." Those profiles "outline results in different categories and threat vectors respective of their decisions, opinions and knowledge throughout the course." The certificate "becomes available once all of the current training cycle tasks are completed."

So the claim is fewer successful attacks and the published measurement is completion plus a score built from answers, opinions and knowledge given inside the course. In 52 pages, the phrases "click-through" or "click rate" appear in 2 files, and neither carries a number. Across the 86 verified quotations in the evidence file, the number of training-outcome claims that state a measured figure is 0. The effect size is 'visibly'.

Then what the control rests on. The methodology page states the premise: "Cyber readiness requires constant vigilance and adaptability to the threat landscape." The phishing guidance says "To avoid falling victim, you must stay vigilant" and "To verify a link, hover your mouse over it without clicking." The malware guidance says "The best way to protect against malware is by being attentive when browsing the internet." And on the ordering of controls: "Anti-virus software should not be the only thing that a computer user relies on. It should be a backup force in case the user has missed something."

The doctrine criteria sit in a file whose hash is pinned inside the evidence file. On the criterion that prevention must assume zero vigilance, 43 of the 86 verified quotations make prevention depend on the user noticing, 5 are partial, and 0 are aligned. 0 of 52 files match the fail-closed patterns, such as a control that holds even if the user clicks. The terms passkey, FIDO, WebAuthn and phishing-resistant appear in 0 of 52 files. Adversary-in-the-middle, MFA fatigue, MFA bypass and session hijacking also appear in 0 files. The MFA options the site lists are "PIN codes sent to the user’s mobile phone, fingerprint scanners, smart cards and specialised hardware tokens", and the reason given is that the account is "more protected against possible database leaks and random intrusion attempts". Smart cards and hardware tokens are listed without saying which kind. The form of MFA that defeats the phish the hover advice is meant to catch is never named as such.

The steelman, in their own words

The full-strength version of RiskSight's position can be assembled from their own pages, and it is a serious one.

Technology misses what training catches: "Conventional security measures rely on detecting known patterns or fingerprints and can therefore be inflexible when it comes to emerging threats or social engineering attacks targeting the human element." The human layer is where breaches happen: "68% of breaches involved a non-malicious human element, like a person falling victim to a social engineering attack or making an error." So instrument it rather than trust it. No pass or fail: "instead of evaluating correct or incorrect answers, we focus on identifying risk areas and evaluating risk. This means that a score of 90% can still mean a potential risk level." Route the measurement into controls: training "must provide clear and actionable outcomes, allowing supervisory staff to implement post-training improvements such as technical measures, operational procedures or additional training to further reduce the possible impact of the human risk behaviour." Respect the limits of people: "Incorrect and unreasonably strict guidance can be demotivating and force users to create shortcuts with potential security vulnerabilities." And, flatly: "Awareness on its own does not build cyber readiness."

That is a risk-quantification programme for a fallible component. It does not claim that people are reliable. It claims that their unreliability can be measured per person and per vector, and that the measurement should drive technical change.

The steelman, on the same evidence

As a measurement argument it holds. The 68% figure, the no-pass/fail design and the routing of results into "technical measures" are all consistent with treating the human as a failure-prone part. That is the right premise.

As a prevention argument it does not get there, for three reasons that the snapshot itself supplies.

First, the measurement is of the wrong quantity. A risk score built from "decisions, opinions and knowledge throughout the course" is a self-report under no adversarial pressure. The Ho study measured exactly the gap between that kind of training state and behaviour under a live simulated attack, and found no significant relationship. A programme that wants to claim "cuts click-through" needs to measure click-through, under attack, against a baseline, over time. The snapshot describes no such measurement.

Second, the site knows people route around friction, and draws no architectural conclusion from it. The shortcuts sentence is correct. The engineering response to it is a control that still holds when the shortcut is taken: a passkey that cannot be relayed, a payment that needs a second approver, a token bound to the device. In 52 pages, 0 sentences describe a passkey, a second approver or a device-bound token. The one use of "fail-safe" concerns redundant systems for disaster recovery, which is recovery, not prevention.

Third, the "inflexible when it comes to emerging threats" premise only supports human detection if humans catch what technology missed. The five phishing indicators the site teaches are headed "Sender Address", a link to hover over, "Suspicious Attachments", "Sense of Urgency" and the content of the email. Sender, destination and attachment are properties a mail gateway, a browser and an origin-bound authenticator check without a human, at a failure rate that does not depend on how tired the human is. That sentence is my inference and not a quotation. It is also why the ordering in "a backup force in case the user has missed something" reads inverted to an engineer: the mechanism is the primary control and the human is the backup, because the mechanism's failure rate is measurable and the human's shows up in 68% of breaches.

What they get right

Three things in the snapshot deserve to be said plainly.

No pass or fail. "it is not possible to fail the dynamic cyber hygiene course." A quiz pass is a claim of verification with nothing behind it, and dropping it is honest.

The DBIR framing. Leading with "68% of breaches involved a non-malicious human element" is leading with the number that argues against your own product's sufficiency.

The shortcuts point, and its cousin: "Taking courses that are too extensive for the attention span can lead to “phantom learners” where the material is simply “clicked through” while actually not learning much." That is the completion metric being criticised from inside a platform that sells completion. It is a correct and uncomfortable observation.

A test a buyer can put to any vendor

Four questions, each with a falsifiable answer. A vendor who cannot answer them is selling evidence of completion.

1. What outcome is measured? Not completion, not a quiz, not a self-assessed score. The rate at which staff take the dangerous action under a realistic attack.

2. Against what baseline? The same rate, in the same population, before the programme, or in a randomised control group running alongside it.

3. Under what adversarial conditions? Simulations designed by someone whose job is to get through, including the lures that work in Estonia today: a call from the bank, a Smart-ID prompt, an invoice from a known supplier.

4. What control holds when the employee fails? The thing that stops the loss after the click, and whether the programme's results feed into it.

Scored on the snapshot, as of 2026-11-12:

1. Outcome measured: completion and an in-course risk level ("Progress is measured on a completion and risk basis"). The click-through claim carries no figure. Not met.

2. Baseline: the reference case states "a visibly decreasing risk level" with no number and no comparison group. Not met.

3. Adversarial conditions: the site says "Conducting periodic phishing simulations tests employees’ knowledge in a practical context." Phishing simulations are named in 5 of 52 files; results from them appear in 0. Partly met.

4. Control that holds on failure: 0 of 52 files match the fail-closed patterns, and 0 of 86 quotations are aligned on that criterion. The results are routed to "technical measures, operational procedures or additional training", which is the right direction and is left open. Not met.

One out of four, partly. The score is of the public methodology. A customer may hold data the site does not publish, and the right of reply below asks for exactly that.

Limits, and the right of reply

Only English URLs were fetched. The absence counts search all 52 files; the quotations come from 22 of them. The doctrine labels on individual quotations are my reading, and a different reader could move some. The counts that carry the argument are far from any boundary: 43 vigilance-dependent quotations against 0 that design for user failure.

A short email with the questions that would change each conclusion has been drafted and is held for the author's decision. If RiskSight publishes outcome data measured under adversarial conditions, this page will carry it as an addendum, dated.
