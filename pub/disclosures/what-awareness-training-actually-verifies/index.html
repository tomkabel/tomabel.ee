Essay · Awareness Training · NIS2 / DORA

What awareness training actually verifies

Cyber awareness training is bought as evidence of resilience. NIS2 and DORA make training and awareness part of the control environment, but the cited provisions do not define a phishing effectiveness metric. The RiskSight public methodology reviewed here documents completion and an in-course risk score; those measures do not, by themselves, show what users do under attack or whether losses are contained. Ho et al. found no significant relationship between recent training completion and later simulation failure, while Lain et al. found that the embedded training they studied did not improve resilience in that deployment. A worked example from an Estonian vendor whose public methodology is detailed enough to test.

The claim and the measurement

A security control has two descriptions. One is what it claims to verify. The other is what it actually verifies. Most of the work on this site lives in the gap between the two, usually at the protocol or opcode layer. This piece applies the same question to a control that runs on people.

The causal chain is simple: delivery proves participation; an in-course score describes responses to the course; a simulated attack measures behaviour under a defined test; a containment or loss measure shows whether the wider control system prevented harm. The public RiskSight material documents the first two.

Some awareness programmes are presented as part of a resilience or human-risk programme. The buyer has to identify the claimed outcome: participation, knowledge, reporting, behaviour under attack or reduced loss. The reviewed public material documents a task being opened, a task being completed, an answer being given and an in-course risk score being computed. None of these is an attack outcome, and the material does not show whether one is measured.

This matters in Estonia for a plain reason. The Police and Border Guard Board counted 3,685 victims of fraud in 2025 and over 29 million euros taken from them, against 16 million in 2024, a near doubling. The largest categories were phone scams at about 11.5 million euros, investment fraud at 6.3 million, frauds aimed at businesses at 2.7 million and sales fraud at 1.7 million. The police release gives no split by victim type. The 2026 yearbook of the Information System Authority (RIA) says: "Individuals are most frequently targeted, but by loss amount, business-related fraud causes the most significant financial damage."

The organisational link also comes from RIA. The yearbook reports a sharp increase in phone scams, affecting both private individuals and company representatives, and says that scams targeting businesses "often result in losses of hundreds of thousands of euros, with little chance of recovery". The mechanism it names is the language barrier: "Previously, the language barrier offered at least some protection. In 2025, that defence collapsed." Poor Estonian grammar or the use of Russian, once a way to spot fraud, can no longer be relied on.

The yearbook's total matches the police figure, so it should not be read as a second measurement. RIA describes it as "three times more than the year before", where the police's own comparison, 16 million to 29 million, is a near doubling. The national awareness effort is context, not a controlled test. The media published hundreds of articles and RIA, the police and banks ran awareness campaigns, and RIA reports that, despite this, fraudsters made larger gains than ever before. This essay asks what the organisational control bought against that loss actually verifies.

What the law asks for

NIS2 requires essential and important entities to take measures that include "basic cyber hygiene practices and cybersecurity training" (Article 21(2)(g)). It also requires Member States to ensure that members of management bodies are required to "follow training", and to encourage entities to offer similar training to employees "on a regular basis" (Article 20(2)). DORA requires financial entities to "develop ICT security awareness programmes and digital operational resilience training as compulsory modules in their staff training schemes" (Article 13(6)).

The training clauses set no phishing failure rate or other effectiveness test. But training is listed among the risk-management measures, and the same article requires, at point (f), "policies and procedures to assess the effectiveness of cybersecurity risk-management measures". The directive asks for training and, separately, for a way to know whether measures work. It does not say how.

For the entity types covered by Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2024/2690, the link is explicit. These are DNS service providers, TLD registries, cloud and data centre providers, content delivery networks, managed service and managed security service providers, online marketplaces, online search engines, social networking platforms and trust service providers. Annex point 8.1.3, the general awareness duty, says "The awareness raising programme shall, where appropriate, be tested in terms of effectiveness." Point 8.2.3 adds that the role-specific security training under point 8.2.1, for staff in roles that need security skills, must be relevant to the job function and that "its effectiveness shall be assessed". The qualifier "where appropriate" in point 8.1.3 is a documented exception: under Article 2(2), an entity that does not apply a requirement must document its reasoning.

NIS2 is a directive. In Estonia it applies through amendments to the Cybersecurity Act (Küberturvalisuse seadus), in force since 1 January 2026, with a three-year transition period for providers to bring their operations into line, and supervised by RIA.

For financial entities DORA is the sector-specific act: recital 28 of NIS2 says that DORA "should be considered to be a sector-specific Union legal act in relation to this Directive with regard to financial entities". DORA pairs the training duty in Article 13(6) with the management body's duty to keep the knowledge and skills to understand and assess ICT risk (Article 5(4)) and with a digital operational resilience testing programme (Article 24). Article 13 also requires financial entities to draw lessons from resilience testing and from incidents. None of these sets a phishing click rate.

A completion record documents that training happened. It does not by itself answer the effectiveness duty in Article 21(2)(f) or, where it applies, point 8.1.3.

What the field studies measure instead

Lain, Kostiainen and Čapkun ran a 15-month study at a partner company from July 2019 to October 2020, with 14,733 enrolled employees, published at the IEEE Symposium on Security and Privacy in 2022 (the arXiv preprint of December 2021 is the open copy). They measured click rates, credential submission and macro enabling on simulated phishing in the employees' normal work context. On embedded training, the kind shown to an employee right after they click a simulated phish, their finding is stated without hedging: it "does not make employees more resilient to phishing, but instead it can have unexpected side effects that can make employees even more susceptible to phishing."

Two things did work in that study. Warnings on suspicious emails were effective. And a reporting button turned the workforce into a detection sensor: the authors say new campaigns can be detected "within few minutes from their launch", and the employees kept reporting over the full 15 months. The data behind that claim is that about 10% of reports arrived within 5 minutes.

Ho and colleagues ran an 8-month randomised controlled experiment at a large healthcare organisation, with ten simulated phishing campaigns sent to over 19,500 employees, published at the IEEE Symposium on Security and Privacy in 2025; all the data come from that one organisation. The abstract reports "no significant relationship between whether users have recently completed cybersecurity awareness training and their likelihood of failing a phishing simulation." For embedded training, "the absolute difference in failure rates between trained and untrained users is extremely low across a variety of training content." Most users "spend minimal time interacting with embedded phishing training material", and for some content types, users who completed more training "can have an increased likelihood of failing subsequent phishing simulations." The authors' conclusion is that such programmes "in their current and commonly deployed forms, are unlikely to offer significant practical value in reducing phishing risks."

The full paper gives the numbers behind that. Time since last training has no significant association with failure (odds ratio 0.998 per 30 days, p = 0.06). Embedded training is statistically significant but small: 9.5% lower odds of failure, which the authors translate to a 1.7 percentage-point absolute difference in the aggregate failure rate. In over 75% of the training sessions, users spent less than 1 minute on the training page. The lure moved failure rates far more than training did: average failure by lure ranged from 1.8% to 30.8%, and 56% of the 19,789 employees in the cleaned data failed at least once over the eight months.

Lain, Jost, Matetic, Kostiainen and Čapkun followed up at ACM CCS 2024 with 4,554 participants, each sent three simulated phishing emails over 6 weeks, plus 25 interviews. On embedded training they report that "its effectiveness comes from its nudging effect, i.e., the periodic reminder of the threat rather than from its content, which is rarely consumed by employees due to lack of time and perceived usefulness." They also find that "delaying training to ease time pressure is as effective as currently established practices, while rewards do not improve secure behavior." Their summary is that "phishing is an attention problem, rather than a knowledge one, even for the most susceptible employees". The authors list limits: nudges were never tested in isolation from training material, the study ran six weeks with three emails, and training might need more time to show an effect.

Between them, these studies tested annual awareness training and embedded post-click training. RiskSight sells a different format: frequent short tasks, described as "Customer-driven training cycles, with up to 52 tasks per year. A task takes 3–5 minutes to complete." Its methodology itself rejects annual campaigns: "The most commonly delivered annual campaign-form cyber awareness courses lack speed for threat adequacy". Neither study tested frequent short tasks, so they do not refute that format.

What they show is narrower. Plausible formats showed little or no benefit when measured, and the human behaviour that produced value in Lain 2022 was reporting. A simulated click rate depends heavily on the lure (1.8% to 30.8% in Ho et al.), so it is a proxy whose difficulty must be held constant. A new format is a hypothesis until it is measured the same way, as the rate of a dangerous action under simulated attack, against a baseline. Completion rate and an in-course score derived from user responses are not that number.

RiskSight, as published on risksight.io on 2026-11-12

This evaluates RiskSight's published methodology for its employee training platform, not private customer data, undisclosed product features, a penetration test or a full implementation audit. The separate executive exercise product ("Moderator-led session-based simulations", "Delivered through the STRATEX Platform") is out of scope. Anything not in English is also out of scope. Only the English pages were fetched, and the product runs in eight languages ("English, Estonian, Finnish, German, Russian, Latvian, Lithuanian, Swedish."), so Estonian-language courseware was not reviewed.

RiskSight's About page gives its address as "Pirni 7/1, Tallinn 10617, Estonia". This site is run by ProksiAbel OÜ, my security consultancy, and adversarial testing of the kind question 3 asks for is adjacent to that work. I have no affiliation with RiskSight, commercial or personal. They are the example here because they are an Estonian vendor whose public methodology is detailed enough to test, which is a compliment to their transparency.

The evidence is a frozen snapshot of 52 English pages from risksight.io, fetched on 2026-11-12. Every sentence attributed to RiskSight below is a verbatim quotation from that snapshot, matched by script, or a count over it. Nothing here names their clients, although the home page lists several. Nothing is said about revenue, staff or motives, because the snapshot says nothing checkable about those.

Start with what the product is sold as. The home page reads: "Your partner for building the human firewall." The product page lists six benefits, and four matter here. Attack prevention: "Regular cyber hygiene training cuts click-through on malicious emails and credential reuse." Reporting, under the heading "A security culture that holds": "Staff report a suspicious message instead of deleting it quietly." Incident cost: "Incidents detected early by employees cost a fraction of those addressed later." Compliance evidence, headed "Evidence for NIS2, DORA and ISO 27001" and explained as "A documented, continuous programme that showcases compliance to auditors and supervisory authorities."

The reference case on the home page is an "Annual cyber awareness training programme since 2017 in six countries for 500 to 700 users with a visibly decreasing risk level." The product page offers "Weekly to Annual Cycles", so an annual programme is a permitted configuration. The showcased case is an annual programme, and the methodology page says that the "most commonly delivered annual campaign-form cyber awareness courses lack speed for threat adequacy".

Now what is measured. The product FAQ answers the question directly: "Progress is measured on a completion and risk basis. Analysts monitor completion rates and overdue users are flagged and notified. Similarly, analysts monitor risk levels and are notified of high-risk users." The risk level comes from inside the course: "Risk is assessed in 12 threat vectors which are categorised into four classes – knowledge, exposure, belonging, and personality." Those profiles "outline results in different categories and threat vectors respective of their decisions, opinions and knowledge throughout the course." The certificate "becomes available once all of the current training cycle tasks are completed."

The product page describes in-browser tasks. Phishing simulations appear only in Resource Centre articles, such as "Conducting periodic phishing simulations tests employees’ knowledge in a practical context." The product page does not describe a phishing-simulation feature, a report button or any reporting metric.

Three of the six benefits are behavioural or loss outcomes: fewer successful attacks, reporting and lower incident cost. The published measurement is completion plus an in-course score built from responses, opinions and knowledge given inside the course. In 52 pages, the phrases "click-through" or "click rate" appear in 2 files, and neither carries a number. One of the two is RiskSight's own article on training challenges, which names "tracking things like phishing simulation click rates" as a way to see whether training works; the public material shows no such figure. No page states a training outcome as a measured number. The reference case describes a "visibly decreasing risk level" but supplies no numeric effect estimate, baseline or comparison group.

Then what the control rests on. The methodology page states the premise: "Cyber readiness requires constant vigilance and adaptability to the threat landscape." The phishing guidance says "To avoid falling victim, you must stay vigilant" and "To verify a link, hover your mouse over it without clicking." The malware guidance says "The best way to protect against malware is by being attentive when browsing the internet." And on the ordering of controls: "Anti-virus software should not be the only thing that a computer user relies on. It should be a backup force in case the user has missed something."

RiskSight sells training, not identity, so the absence of identity products is not the point. The site does cover access control: "Implementing the principle of least functionality and least privilege helps minimise attack surfaces and limit potential vulnerabilities." The point is what the published guidance says about logins. The MFA options it lists are "PIN codes sent to the user’s mobile phone, fingerprint scanners, smart cards and specialised hardware tokens", and the reason given is that the account is "more protected against possible database leaks and random intrusion attempts". Nothing separates codes that can be phished from authenticators bound to the site's origin, and smart cards and hardware tokens are listed without saying which kind.

The terms passkey, FIDO, WebAuthn and phishing-resistant appear in 0 of 52 files. Adversary-in-the-middle, MFA fatigue, MFA bypass and session hijacking also appear in 0. So the Resource Centre advice asks users to catch credential phish that an origin-bound authenticator would make harmless, and never mentions that option.

The strongest case for awareness training

The full-strength version of RiskSight's position can be assembled from their own pages, and it is a serious one.

Technology misses what training catches: "Conventional security measures rely on detecting known patterns or fingerprints and can therefore be inflexible when it comes to emerging threats or social engineering attacks targeting the human element." The human layer is where breaches happen, and RiskSight cites Verizon's 2024 Data Breach Investigations Report for it: "68% of breaches involved a non-malicious human element, like a person falling victim to a social engineering attack or making an error." So instrument it rather than trust it. No pass or fail: "instead of evaluating correct or incorrect answers, we focus on identifying risk areas and evaluating risk. This means that a score of 90% can still mean a potential risk level." Route the measurement into controls: training "must provide clear and actionable outcomes, allowing supervisory staff to implement post-training improvements such as technical measures, operational procedures or additional training to further reduce the possible impact of the human risk behaviour." Respect the limits of people: "Incorrect and unreasonably strict guidance can be demotivating and force users to create shortcuts with potential security vulnerabilities." And, flatly: "Awareness on its own does not build cyber readiness."

That is a risk-quantification programme for a fallible component. It does not claim that people are reliable. It claims that their unreliability can be measured per person and per vector, and that the measurement should drive technical change.

Where the argument stops

As a measurement argument it holds. The 68% figure, the no-pass/fail design and the routing of results into "technical measures" are all consistent with treating the human as a failure-prone part. That is the right premise.

As a prevention argument it does not get there, for three reasons that the snapshot itself supplies.

First, the measurement is of the wrong quantity. On the public description, the score is derived from responses made inside the course rather than from a published adversarial attack outcome. The public material does not show how strongly that score predicts behaviour under attack. Ho et al. tested a closely related gap: whether recent completion of commonly deployed awareness training predicted behaviour during subsequent simulated attacks, and found no significant relationship. A programme that wants to claim "cuts click-through" needs to measure click-through, under attack, against a baseline, over time. The snapshot describes no such measurement. The reporting and incident-cost benefits would need a reporting rate, a time to first report and a containment time, and no page documents any of them.

Second, the site names the shortcut problem and says results should drive "technical measures", but publishes no example of a control that holds when the user fails. The shortcuts sentence is correct. The engineering response to it is a control that still holds when the shortcut is taken: a passkey that cannot be relayed, a payment that needs a second approver, a token bound to the device.

Third, the "inflexible when it comes to emerging threats" premise only supports human detection if humans catch what technology missed, and the five phishing indicators the published guidance lists are headed "Sender Address", a link to hover over, "Suspicious Attachments", "Sense of Urgency" and the content of the email. Mail gateways, browsers and origin-bound authenticators can check important properties without relying on perfect human attention, and although they do not close every social-engineering path, they take whole classes of decision off the human failure path.

The 68% figure also counts more than phishing: in Verizon's own 2024 definition it covers a person "making an error or falling prey to a social engineering attack". Verizon's 2025 report puts human involvement at 60% and recalculates 2024 to 61%: "we see the human involvement in breaches at 60% this year, as opposed to 61% last year". Verizon calls the 2025 figure a "slightly more precise" calculation, so 68% to 61% is mostly a method change; the share is about 60% in both years. The figure does not isolate training, but a share that size supports designing for human failure rather than relying on human detection.

What holds when the person fails

No single control covers every path. The paths that recur in the RIA and police material are phishing links that collect card details or logins, changed supplier bank details and the coached phone call, and each needs a different control.

A phishing link, often posing as a parcel or delivery notice, sends the victim to a look-alike page that collects card details or a login. An authenticator bound to the site's origin, such as a passkey, FIDO2 or WebAuthn, or the Estonian ID card, does not release a usable login to a look-alike site, even if the user clicks. It does nothing for card details typed into a fake page, which is why the other controls matter.

Changed supplier bank details, the pattern known as business email compromise, is a different case. A strong login does not stop a logged-in employee from paying a real-looking invoice from a compromised supplier mailbox. The control is a second approver enforced by the payment system, and a call-back on a number already on file whenever bank details change.

The coached phone call is the third. The caller talks the victim through entering PIN codes, making a transfer or installing a remote desktop tool, and RIA describes cases involving AnyDesk. For Smart-ID+, the updated version of Smart-ID, RIA makes a narrow claim: "While it is no silver bullet against all scams, it is expected to slow down some phone scams that pressure users to enter PIN codes." The controls are blocking unapproved remote-access tools on company devices, holds or limits on unusual transfers, and a one-step way to report.

Reporting is where the evidence for training is least weak. Verizon's 2025 report found that users with more recent training reported simulated phishing at "about 21% against a base rate of 5%", while the click-rate effect was much smaller; the comparison is observational. In Lain 2022, new campaigns could be detected within minutes from reports. Ask people for early reports, not perfect detection.

What they get right

Three things in the snapshot deserve to be said plainly.

No pass or fail: "it is not possible to fail the dynamic cyber hygiene course." A quiz pass can verify recall, not behaviour during an attack, and dropping the label avoids false assurance.

The Verizon figure. Leading with it argues against the product's own sufficiency.

The shortcuts point, and its cousin: "Taking courses that are too extensive for the attention span can lead to “phantom learners” where the material is simply “clicked through” while actually not learning much." That is the completion metric being criticised from inside a platform that sells completion.

A test a buyer can put to any vendor

Five questions: four for the vendor and one for the buyer's own architecture, each with an answer that can be checked. A vendor who cannot answer the first four can substantiate coverage and completion, but cannot substantiate the relevant security outcome from the public evidence reviewed.

1. What outcome do you claim, and what do you measure for it? Compliance evidence, knowledge, reporting, behaviour under attack or loss. The measure should match the claim.

2. Against what baseline? The same population before the programme, or a randomised control group running alongside it, with lure difficulty held constant. In Ho et al., the lure moved failure rates from 1.8% to 30.8%.

3. Under what adversarial conditions? Simulations designed by someone whose job is to get through, using lures that work in Estonia now, in fluent Estonian: a call from the bank, a Smart-ID prompt, an invoice from a known supplier.

4. Do you measure reporting? Reporting rate, time to first report, and how many reports the security team can act on.

5. For the buyer, not the vendor: what control holds in our own environment when the employee fails, and do the programme's results change it?

Scored on the snapshot, as of 2026-11-12:

1. Met for compliance evidence only. Completion and in-course risk are documented; the attack, reporting and incident-cost benefits carry no figure.

2. Not met. "a visibly decreasing risk level" has no number, and it describes the in-course risk score, not an attack outcome.

3. Not assessable for Estonian conditions, because only English pages were reviewed. No English page describes adversarially designed simulations as part of the product, and simulation results appear in 0 files.

4. Not met. Reporting is marketed ("Staff report a suspicious message instead of deleting it quietly.") but no page documents a reporting metric.

Question 5 belongs to the buyer. RiskSight routes results toward "technical measures, operational procedures or additional training", which is the right direction, but whether a control holds is decided in the buyer's environment, not on a vendor's website.

On the public record, one of four vendor questions is met, and only for compliance evidence; one cannot be assessed from English pages. This applies to the published material only. A customer may hold data the site does not publish.

Limits, and the right of reply

The absence counts search all 52 files, and every quotation attributed to RiskSight comes from the snapshot. An absence of published outcome figures is not evidence of zero effect. It means a buyer cannot verify the preventive claim from the public record.

The assessment should be updated if RiskSight publishes outcome data measured under adversarial conditions or supplies evidence that changes a conclusion. The public counts describe what a buyer can verify from the frozen methodology; they do not establish what controls a customer may deploy with the product. Corrections and replies can be sent to tom@tomabel.ee. A reply from RiskSight will be published in full as a dated addendum.
